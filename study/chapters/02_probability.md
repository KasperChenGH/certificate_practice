# Chapter 2 — Probability Review

This chapter is a terse — but fully rigorous — review of the probabilistic machinery used throughout the document. The reader is assumed to have prior exposure to measure-theoretic probability; nothing here is introduced from scratch. Each result is stated precisely and proved in full when it is invoked later in the text. The keystone result of the chapter is the closed-form expression for $\mathbb{E}\big[(S_T - K)^+\big]$ when $S_T$ is lognormal — this is the engine that drives the Black-Scholes-Merton formula in Chapter 8.

## Goals

- Recall the measure-theoretic setup — probability spaces, filtrations, conditional expectation — that will be used without further comment in later chapters.
- State and prove the moment generating function identity for normal random variables, which is the technical lemma behind every lognormal computation that follows.
- Compute $\mathbb{E}\big[(S_T - K)^+\big]$ in closed form when $\log S_T$ is normal. This single expression, with $m$ replaced by the risk-free rate $r$, is the Black-Scholes call formula.
- Establish the notational and proof-style conventions that the rest of the document will rely on: every formula is annotated by a `where` block, and every theorem is followed by a complete proof unless explicitly labeled a sketch.

## Prerequisites

Chapter 1 (notation, conventions, the filtered probability space $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P}, \{\mathcal{F}_t\}_{t \ge 0})$); familiarity with sigma-algebras, Lebesgue integration, and conditional expectation at the level of Shreve's *Stochastic Calculus for Finance II*, Chapters 1–2.

## Probability space and random variables

### Definition

A **probability space** is a triple $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})$, where $\Omega$ is the sample space (a set of outcomes), $\mathcal{F}$ is a **sigma-algebra** on $\Omega$ (a collection of subsets of $\Omega$ closed under complements and countable unions, containing $\Omega$), and $\mathbb{P}: \mathcal{F} \to [0, 1]$ is a **probability measure** (countably additive, with $\mathbb{P}(\Omega) = 1$). A **random variable** is a function $X: \Omega \to \mathbb{R}$ that is $\mathcal{F}$-measurable, i.e., $\{X \le x\} \in \mathcal{F}$ for every $x \in \mathbb{R}$.

::: where
- $\Omega$ — sample space, the set of all possible outcomes
- $\mathcal{F}$ — sigma-algebra of events on $\Omega$
- $\mathbb{P}$ — probability measure, assigning a number in $[0,1]$ to every event in $\mathcal{F}$
- $X$ — random variable, a measurable real-valued function on $\Omega$
- $\{X \le x\}$ — the event $\{\omega \in \Omega : X(\omega) \le x\}$
:::

### Definition

For $p \ge 1$, the space $L^p(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})$ consists of all (equivalence classes of) random variables $X$ with $\mathbb{E}[|X|^p] < \infty$, equipped with the norm $\|X\|_p = \mathbb{E}[|X|^p]^{1/p}$. We use $L^1$ for integrable random variables and $L^2$ for square-integrable ones. Stochastic integration in Chapter 3 is built on the Hilbert space structure of $L^2$.

::: where
- $L^p$ — Lebesgue space of $p$-integrable random variables on $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})$
- $\mathbb{E}[|X|^p]$ — expectation of $|X|^p$ under $\mathbb{P}$
- $\|X\|_p$ — the $L^p$ norm of $X$
:::

## Filtration and conditional expectation

### Definition

A **filtration** on $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})$ is an increasing family $\{\mathcal{F}_t\}_{t \ge 0}$ of sub-sigma-algebras of $\mathcal{F}$: $\mathcal{F}_s \subseteq \mathcal{F}_t \subseteq \mathcal{F}$ whenever $s \le t$. Intuitively, $\mathcal{F}_t$ encodes the information available to the market at time $t$. A stochastic process $\{X_t\}_{t \ge 0}$ is **adapted** to $\{\mathcal{F}_t\}$ if $X_t$ is $\mathcal{F}_t$-measurable for every $t$; it is **predictable** if it is measurable with respect to the sigma-algebra on $\Omega \times [0, \infty)$ generated by left-continuous adapted processes.

::: where
- $\{\mathcal{F}_t\}_{t \ge 0}$ — filtration, the information flow over time
- $\mathcal{F}_t$ — sigma-algebra of events observable by time $t$
- $X_t$ — value of a stochastic process at time $t$
- adapted — $X_t$ depends only on information up to time $t$
- predictable — $X_t$ depends only on information strictly before time $t$
:::

### Definition

Let $X \in L^1(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})$ and let $\mathcal{G}$ be a sub-sigma-algebra of $\mathcal{F}$. The **conditional expectation** $\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]$ is the (almost-surely unique) $\mathcal{G}$-measurable random variable satisfying the orthogonality property
$$\int_A X \, d\mathbb{P} = \int_A \mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}] \, d\mathbb{P} \qquad \text{for every } A \in \mathcal{G}.$$

::: where
- $X$ — integrable random variable on $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})$
- $\mathcal{G}$ — sub-sigma-algebra of $\mathcal{F}$ (the conditioning information)
- $\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]$ — conditional expectation: $\mathcal{G}$-measurable, almost-surely unique
- $A$ — any event in $\mathcal{G}$
- $\int_A \cdot \, d\mathbb{P}$ — Lebesgue integral over the event $A$ with respect to $\mathbb{P}$
:::

Existence and uniqueness follow from the Radon-Nikodym theorem applied to the signed measure $A \mapsto \int_A X \, d\mathbb{P}$ on $(\Omega, \mathcal{G})$, which is absolutely continuous with respect to $\mathbb{P}|_\mathcal{G}$.

### Theorem (Tower property)

Let $\mathcal{G} \subseteq \mathcal{H} \subseteq \mathcal{F}$ be sub-sigma-algebras, and let $X \in L^1$. Then
$$\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{H}] \mid \mathcal{G}\big] = \mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}] \quad \text{a.s.}$$

::: where
- $\mathcal{G}, \mathcal{H}$ — sub-sigma-algebras with $\mathcal{G} \subseteq \mathcal{H} \subseteq \mathcal{F}$
- $X$ — integrable random variable
- $\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{H}]$ — conditional expectation of $X$ given the finer information $\mathcal{H}$
- $\mathbb{E}[\,\cdot \mid \mathcal{G}]$ — outer conditional expectation, given the coarser information $\mathcal{G}$
:::

### Proof sketch

Set $Y = \mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{H}]$ and $Z = \mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]$. We must show $\mathbb{E}[Y \mid \mathcal{G}] = Z$. By the defining orthogonality property of $\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{H}]$, for any $A \in \mathcal{H}$,
$$\int_A Y \, d\mathbb{P} = \int_A X \, d\mathbb{P}.$$
Since $\mathcal{G} \subseteq \mathcal{H}$, every $A \in \mathcal{G}$ is also in $\mathcal{H}$, so the equality holds for $A \in \mathcal{G}$. But the defining property of $Z$ also gives $\int_A Z \, d\mathbb{P} = \int_A X \, d\mathbb{P}$ for every $A \in \mathcal{G}$. Therefore $\int_A Y \, d\mathbb{P} = \int_A Z \, d\mathbb{P}$ for all $A \in \mathcal{G}$. Since $Z$ is $\mathcal{G}$-measurable and the conditional expectation is uniquely characterized by this orthogonality property, $\mathbb{E}[Y \mid \mathcal{G}] = Z$. $\blacksquare$

## Normal and lognormal distributions

### Definition

A random variable $Z$ has the **standard normal distribution**, written $Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$, if its probability density and cumulative distribution functions are
$$\varphi(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\, e^{-x^2/2}, \qquad N(x) = \int_{-\infty}^x \varphi(u) \, du.$$
A random variable $X$ has the **normal distribution with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$**, written $X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$, if $X = \mu + \sigma Z$ for some $Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$.

::: where
- $Z$ — standard normal random variable
- $x, u$ — real-valued dummy variables of integration
- $\varphi(x)$ — standard normal probability density function
- $N(x)$ — standard normal cumulative distribution function (also written $\Phi$ in some sources)
- $\mu$ — mean of $X$ (real number)
- $\sigma > 0$ — standard deviation of $X$; $\sigma^2$ is the variance
- $X$ — normal random variable with parameters $(\mu, \sigma^2)$
:::

### Theorem (Normal MGF)

If $X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$, then
$$\mathbb{E}[e^X] = e^{\mu + \sigma^2/2}.$$

::: where
- $X$ — normal random variable with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$
- $\mu$ — mean of $X$
- $\sigma^2$ — variance of $X$
- $\mathbb{E}[\,\cdot\,]$ — expectation under $\mathbb{P}$
- $e^{\,\cdot\,}$ — natural exponential function
:::

### Proof

Write the expectation as an integral against the normal density:
$$\mathbb{E}[e^X] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{x} \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\, \exp\!\left(-\frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right) dx.$$

Combine the two exponentials and expand:
$$x - \frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2} = -\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\Big[(x - \mu)^2 - 2\sigma^2 x\Big] = -\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\Big[x^2 - 2(\mu + \sigma^2) x + \mu^2\Big].$$

Complete the square in $x$:
$$x^2 - 2(\mu + \sigma^2) x + \mu^2 = \big(x - (\mu + \sigma^2)\big)^2 - (\mu + \sigma^2)^2 + \mu^2.$$

Expanding the trailing terms:
$$-(\mu + \sigma^2)^2 + \mu^2 = -\mu^2 - 2\mu\sigma^2 - \sigma^4 + \mu^2 = -2\mu\sigma^2 - \sigma^4 = -2\sigma^2\!\left(\mu + \tfrac{\sigma^2}{2}\right).$$

Therefore the integrand exponent is
$$-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\big(x - (\mu + \sigma^2)\big)^2 + \left(\mu + \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\right).$$

Pull the constant factor $e^{\mu + \sigma^2/2}$ out of the integral:
$$\mathbb{E}[e^X] = e^{\mu + \sigma^2/2} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\, \exp\!\left(-\frac{(x - (\mu + \sigma^2))^2}{2\sigma^2}\right) dx.$$

The remaining integrand is the density of a $\mathcal{N}(\mu + \sigma^2, \sigma^2)$ random variable, which integrates to $1$. Hence $\mathbb{E}[e^X] = e^{\mu + \sigma^2/2}$. $\blacksquare$

### Corollary (Lognormal moments)

If $Y = e^X$ with $X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$, then $Y$ has the **lognormal distribution** with parameters $(\mu, \sigma^2)$, and
$$\mathbb{E}[Y] = e^{\mu + \sigma^2/2}, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(Y) = e^{2\mu + \sigma^2}\!\left(e^{\sigma^2} - 1\right).$$

::: where
- $X$ — underlying normal with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$
- $Y = e^X$ — lognormal random variable
- $\mu$ — mean of $\log Y$ (not of $Y$ itself)
- $\sigma^2$ — variance of $\log Y$
- $\mathbb{E}[Y]$ — expectation of $Y$
- $\operatorname{Var}(Y) = \mathbb{E}[Y^2] - (\mathbb{E}[Y])^2$ — variance of $Y$
:::

The first moment is the MGF identity applied directly: $\mathbb{E}[Y] = \mathbb{E}[e^X] = e^{\mu + \sigma^2/2}$. For the variance, observe that $Y^2 = e^{2X}$ and $2X \sim \mathcal{N}(2\mu, 4\sigma^2)$, so applying the MGF to $2X$ gives $\mathbb{E}[Y^2] = e^{2\mu + 2\sigma^2}$. Therefore
$$\operatorname{Var}(Y) = e^{2\mu + 2\sigma^2} - e^{2\mu + \sigma^2} = e^{2\mu + \sigma^2}\!\left(e^{\sigma^2} - 1\right). \blacksquare$$

## Lognormal expected payoff

This is the keystone result of the chapter.

### Theorem

Let
$$S_T = S_0 \exp\!\left(\big(m - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2\big) T + \sigma \sqrt{T}\, Z\right), \qquad Z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1).$$
Then
$$\mathbb{E}\big[(S_T - K)^+\big] = S_0\, e^{mT}\, N(d_1) - K\, N(d_2),$$
where
$$d_1 = \frac{\ln(S_0 / K) + \big(m + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2\big) T}{\sigma \sqrt{T}}, \qquad d_2 = d_1 - \sigma \sqrt{T}.$$

::: where
- $S_0 > 0$ — initial spot price of the underlying (time-zero value)
- $S_T$ — terminal value of the underlying, lognormally distributed
- $T > 0$ — maturity, in years
- $m$ — drift parameter of $\log S_T$ (under whatever measure is in play; in Chapter 8 we take $m = r$)
- $\sigma > 0$ — volatility, the standard deviation of $\log(S_T / S_0)$ per unit $\sqrt{T}$
- $Z$ — standard normal random variable
- $K > 0$ — strike of the call payoff
- $(x)^+$ — positive part, $\max(x, 0)$
- $N(\cdot)$ — standard normal CDF
- $d_1, d_2$ — auxiliary variables defined above; they will reappear as $d_+, d_-$ in Chapter 8
- $\ln$ — natural logarithm
:::

### Proof

We compute the expectation in four steps.

**Step 1: identify the exercise event.** The call is in the money when $S_T > K$. Taking logarithms and using the definition of $S_T$,
$$S_T > K \iff \big(m - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2\big) T + \sigma \sqrt{T}\, Z > \ln(K / S_0) \iff Z > \frac{\ln(K / S_0) - (m - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2) T}{\sigma \sqrt{T}}.$$
The right-hand side equals $-d_2$ since
$$-d_2 = -\frac{\ln(S_0 / K) + (m - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2) T}{\sigma \sqrt{T}} = \frac{\ln(K / S_0) - (m - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2) T}{\sigma \sqrt{T}}.$$
Hence $\{S_T > K\} = \{Z > -d_2\}$.

**Step 2: split the expectation.** Write
$$\mathbb{E}\big[(S_T - K)^+\big] = \mathbb{E}\big[(S_T - K) \mathbf{1}_{S_T > K}\big] = \mathbb{E}\big[S_T \mathbf{1}_{S_T > K}\big] - K\, \mathbb{P}(S_T > K).$$
We evaluate each piece in turn.

**Step 3: the strike-times-probability term.** By symmetry of the standard normal,
$$\mathbb{P}(S_T > K) = \mathbb{P}(Z > -d_2) = \mathbb{P}(Z < d_2) = N(d_2).$$

**Step 4: the stock-times-indicator term.** Substitute the explicit form of $S_T$ and write the expectation as a Gaussian integral over $z$:
$$\mathbb{E}\big[S_T \mathbf{1}_{S_T > K}\big] = \int_{-d_2}^{\infty} S_0\, e^{(m - \sigma^2/2) T + \sigma \sqrt{T}\, z} \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\, e^{-z^2/2}\, dz.$$
Pull $S_0\, e^{(m - \sigma^2/2) T}$ outside and combine the two exponentials in the integrand:
$$\sigma \sqrt{T}\, z - \frac{z^2}{2} = -\frac{1}{2}\big(z^2 - 2 \sigma \sqrt{T}\, z\big) = -\frac{1}{2}\big(z - \sigma \sqrt{T}\big)^2 + \frac{\sigma^2 T}{2}.$$
Pull the constant $e^{\sigma^2 T / 2}$ outside:
$$\mathbb{E}\big[S_T \mathbf{1}_{S_T > K}\big] = S_0\, e^{(m - \sigma^2/2) T + \sigma^2 T / 2} \int_{-d_2}^{\infty} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\, e^{-(z - \sigma \sqrt{T})^2 / 2}\, dz = S_0\, e^{mT} \int_{-d_2}^{\infty} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\, e^{-(z - \sigma \sqrt{T})^2 / 2}\, dz.$$
Substitute $u = z - \sigma \sqrt{T}$, so $du = dz$ and the lower limit becomes $u = -d_2 - \sigma \sqrt{T} = -d_1$:
$$\mathbb{E}\big[S_T \mathbf{1}_{S_T > K}\big] = S_0\, e^{mT} \int_{-d_1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\, e^{-u^2/2}\, du = S_0\, e^{mT}\, \mathbb{P}(Z > -d_1) = S_0\, e^{mT}\, N(d_1).$$

**Step 5: combine.** Putting steps 3 and 4 into step 2,
$$\mathbb{E}\big[(S_T - K)^+\big] = S_0\, e^{mT}\, N(d_1) - K\, N(d_2). \blacksquare$$

### Remark

This is the engine that drives the closed-form Black-Scholes call price. In Chapter 8 we recover the standard formula by working under the risk-neutral measure $\mathbb{Q}$, under which $\log(S_T / S_0)$ is normal with mean $(r - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2) T$ and variance $\sigma^2 T$ — i.e., $m = r$. The discounted expectation $e^{-rT}\, \mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\big[(S_T - K)^+\big] = S_0\, N(d_1) - K\, e^{-rT}\, N(d_2)$ is then the call price. Every subsequent closed-form result — the put via put-call parity, the digital options, the Greeks — is downstream of this single computation.

## Practice

::: problem [Conceptual]
**Problem 2.1.** Why does the tower property hold? Give the financial interpretation when $\mathcal{G}$ represents today's information and $\mathcal{H}$ represents tomorrow's information, and then derive it from the orthogonality property of conditional expectation.

::: solution
**Financial interpretation.** If $\mathcal{G} \subseteq \mathcal{H}$, then $\mathcal{H}$ is a finer information set than $\mathcal{G}$ — say $\mathcal{G}$ is today's information and $\mathcal{H}$ is tomorrow's. The tower property says: today's best estimate of tomorrow's best estimate of $X$ equals today's best estimate of $X$. Intuitively, conditioning on more information later, then averaging back to today's coarser information, cannot improve on the original today-conditional estimate. This is the abstract formulation of the martingale-like principle that drives risk-neutral pricing: the discounted price today equals today's expectation of the discounted price tomorrow, regardless of how many intermediate time steps are inserted.

**Derivation.** Let $Y = \mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{H}]$ and $Z = \mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]$. Pick any $A \in \mathcal{G}$. Since $\mathcal{G} \subseteq \mathcal{H}$, the event $A$ also lies in $\mathcal{H}$, so the defining orthogonality property of $\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{H}]$ gives
$$\int_A X \, d\mathbb{P} = \int_A Y \, d\mathbb{P}.$$
Simultaneously, the defining property of $\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]$ applied directly gives
$$\int_A X \, d\mathbb{P} = \int_A Z \, d\mathbb{P}.$$
Combining the two equalities, $\int_A Y \, d\mathbb{P} = \int_A Z \, d\mathbb{P}$ for every $A \in \mathcal{G}$. Since $Z$ is $\mathcal{G}$-measurable and conditional expectation is uniquely characterized (up to almost-sure equality) by the orthogonality property, this forces $\mathbb{E}[Y \mid \mathcal{G}] = Z$, i.e., $\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{H}] \mid \mathcal{G}\big] = \mathbb{E}[X \mid \mathcal{G}]$. $\blacksquare$
:::
:::

::: problem [Derivation]
**Problem 2.2.** Prove the MGF identity $\mathbb{E}[e^X] = e^{\mu + \sigma^2/2}$ for $X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$.

::: solution
Write the expectation against the normal density:
$$\mathbb{E}[e^X] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^x \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\, \exp\!\left(-\frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right) dx.$$
The combined exponent is
$$x - \frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2} = -\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\big[x^2 - 2(\mu + \sigma^2) x + \mu^2\big].$$
Completing the square in $x$:
$$x^2 - 2(\mu + \sigma^2) x + \mu^2 = \big(x - (\mu + \sigma^2)\big)^2 + \mu^2 - (\mu + \sigma^2)^2,$$
and the trailing constants simplify as
$$\mu^2 - (\mu + \sigma^2)^2 = -2\mu\sigma^2 - \sigma^4 = -2\sigma^2\!\left(\mu + \tfrac{\sigma^2}{2}\right).$$
Hence
$$-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\big[x^2 - 2(\mu + \sigma^2) x + \mu^2\big] = -\frac{(x - (\mu + \sigma^2))^2}{2\sigma^2} + \left(\mu + \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\right).$$
The constant $e^{\mu + \sigma^2/2}$ pulls outside the integral, and the remaining integrand is the density of a $\mathcal{N}(\mu + \sigma^2, \sigma^2)$ random variable — which integrates to $1$. Therefore $\mathbb{E}[e^X] = e^{\mu + \sigma^2/2}$. $\blacksquare$
:::

::: problem [Computation]
**Problem 2.3.** Let $S_0 = 100$, $\sigma = 0.20$, $m = 0.05$, $T = 1$, $K = 100$. Compute $\mathbb{E}\big[(S_T - K)^+\big]$ to two decimal places using the closed-form theorem of §6. Show the intermediate values $d_1$, $d_2$, $N(d_1)$, $N(d_2)$.

::: solution
Plug the inputs into the formulas for $d_1, d_2$:
$$d_1 = \frac{\ln(S_0 / K) + (m + \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2) T}{\sigma \sqrt{T}} = \frac{\ln(1) + (0.05 + 0.02) \cdot 1}{0.20 \cdot 1} = \frac{0.07}{0.20} = 0.35,$$
$$d_2 = d_1 - \sigma \sqrt{T} = 0.35 - 0.20 = 0.15.$$
Look up (or compute) the standard-normal CDF values:
$$N(d_1) = N(0.35) \approx 0.6368, \qquad N(d_2) = N(0.15) \approx 0.5596.$$
Apply the closed form:
$$\mathbb{E}\big[(S_T - K)^+\big] = S_0\, e^{mT}\, N(d_1) - K\, N(d_2) = 100 \cdot e^{0.05} \cdot 0.6368 - 100 \cdot 0.5596.$$
With $e^{0.05} \approx 1.0513$:
$$= 100 \cdot 1.0513 \cdot 0.6368 - 55.96 \approx 66.95 - 55.96 \approx \$10.99.$$
So $\mathbb{E}\big[(S_T - K)^+\big] \approx \$10.99$. (Under the risk-neutral measure with $m = r$, the call price is $e^{-rT}$ times this, namely $\approx \$10.45$ — but the theorem here gives the undiscounted expected payoff, as stated.) $\blacksquare$
:::
